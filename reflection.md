# Task 2 Reflection

While running the receipt-recognition chain for this FTEC5660 Homework 1, I found that the model could read most of the receipts and calculate the total spending correctly, but it produced different answers for the question about how much I would have paid without discounts. Sometimes it missed only one small discount; at other times it missed more. I eventually had to add independent extraction, a second review pass, a cross-check against the original item total, and `Decimal`-based aggregation. This reminded me of one of Anthropic's parallel multi-agent experiments: spending more tokens can produce more accurate results. The experience made me realize that a model that looks "mostly correct" is not necessarily a system that is ready to deliver. A decimal place, a missed negative amount, or an unverified assumption can determine whether the final result is reliable.

Looking back at the AI developments of the past ten days, I see the same direction. Models are becoming cheaper and faster, while moving beyond text-only question answering toward image editing, real-time translation, audio and video understanding, and tool-using Agents. Reports about Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol/Luna emphasized lower costs; Qwen-Image-2.1 combined image generation and editing in one open-source model; and Qwen3.8-LiveTranslate and Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash pushed real-time language and multimodal tasks further. The news that Xiaohongshu open-sourced the Iris Search Agent model also made me feel that advanced model capabilities are gradually spreading beyond a small number of companies and their APIs. To me, this means that future competition will not simply be about who can call the strongest model. It will be about who can put a model into a real workflow and make it produce useful results consistently.

At the same time, these developments make me unwilling to equate a stronger model with a more reliable system. Reports during the same period about agent vulnerabilities and the use of Grok/Zcode in high-risk activities reminded me that once a model has access to a browser, terminal, files, or external systems, the cost of an error can be much higher than a wrong sentence. This is especially important in business and financial settings, where a system must consider permission boundaries, audit records, privacy, cost, and human confirmation in addition to accuracy. I now prefer to see an Agent as a software component that needs testing and governance.

I would like to work on Agent projects for business or financial workflows, such as document review, customer service, or data analysis, while letting the model make recommendations and leaving critical decisions to rules and human review. AI has not made me feel that people can simply leave the process. Instead, it has made me more interested in becoming someone who can connect model capabilities with real business problems and requirements for reliability.
